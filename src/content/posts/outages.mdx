## The short version

We spend most of our resilience budget on dead hardware. Multi-AZ, replicas, failover. That's sensible, and it's probably why hardware only started 1 in 6 of these outages.

## Six outages worth reading in full

The numbers tell you what. These tell you how. Every quote is copied from the provider's report.

<Exhibit source="aws" date="2017-02-28" title="One wrong input">
The playbook was right. The person was authorised. One argument was wrong, and nothing stopped the command from removing more than it should. Tools that remove capacity need a ceiling they refuse to go past.
</Exhibit>

<Exhibit source="gcp" date="2025-06-12" title="Blank fields, everywhere, in seconds">
This is the one redundancy can't save you from. Replication did its job perfectly: it copied the bad data to every region. Config and policy data need the same staged rollout code gets.
</Exhibit>

<Exhibit source="cloudflare" date="2025-11-18" title="A permissions change that doubled a file">
Nobody touched the bot system. A database permissions change upstream changed what a query returned, and a file downstream doubled. Your blast radius includes everything that reads your output.
</Exhibit>

<Exhibit source="aws" date="2025-10-19" title="The DNS one wasn't really about DNS">
Yes, DNS was where it showed up. But the record went empty because of a race in the automation that manages it, and the automation then failed to repair it. Even in the famous DNS outage, the cause was one layer below.
</Exhibit>

<Exhibit source="aws" date="2020-11-25" title="A small capacity add">
AWS's own words: the trigger, not the root cause. A routine, small change pushed every server in the fleet past an operating system thread limit. Small changes aren't safe changes. They're just changes nobody watches.
</Exhibit>

<Exhibit source="cloudflare" date="2019-07-02" title="One WAF rule, every CPU core">
A non-emergency rule went out worldwide with no staged rollout. One of Cloudflare's fixes afterwards: roll out rules in stages, the same way they roll out software.
</Exhibit>

## What I'd do on Monday

1. **Treat config like code.** Review it, version it, and roll it out one region (or one cell) at a time. Two of the worst ones here, Google Cloud in June 2025 and Cloudflare in 2019, were a push that reached everywhere at once.
2. **Make rollback one command, and practise it.** Nobody learns rollback during the outage.
3. **Put a ceiling on your automation.** A script that removes servers or scales capacity should refuse to touch more than a set share of the fleet without a human.
4. **Watch what reads your output.** Your change can be fine and still break the system downstream that consumes it.
5. **Ask one question first on every incident call:** what changed in the last 2 hours? Keep the change log where on-call can see it.

## How I built this, and what it can't tell you

- **Only published reports.** Providers write these for big, customer-facing incidents. This is big-outage data, not every blip.
- **Fixed rules, no cherry-picking.** The provider table says exactly what I included from each one. My first Azure pass picked 25 of 84 reports and skipped power events, which pushed "change" to about 76%. I redid it with every final report and got 62%.
- **One trigger per outage is a judgement call.** Every row below has the quote and the link, so you can check mine.
- **Checked against the source.** I re-opened 20 random reports and matched the quotes word for word. All 20 matched.
- Some older Cloudflare post-mortems may be missing. Google Cloud only counts incidents that state a cause.
